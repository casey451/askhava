## Arizona classifications that could apply

Arizona's TPT system does not simply impose tax on every dollar a business earns. The relevant question is whether the business activity falls within a taxable classification. The broad definition of “gross income” in A.R.S. §42-5001 includes receipts from services, but that definition describes the measure of income once a taxable classification applies; it does not by itself create a general tax on every service business. The specific classification statutes therefore matter. citeturn41view7turn21search4

### Retail

**[settled] A.R.S. §42-5061 defines the retail classification around the business of selling tangible personal property at retail.** The statute also recognizes the distinction between taxable property sales and service transactions; current §42-5061 contains exclusions for professional or personal services in which any property transfer is only an inconsequential element, and for services rendered in addition to a sale of tangible personal property under the statutory conditions. citeturn21search4

**[analysis] Ask Hava is not ordinarily making a retail sale.** The provider is not buying a physical product from Ask Hava. The homeowner's remodeling, plumbing, HVAC, pool-service, detailing, or other work is sold by the independent provider directly to the homeowner. Ask Hava does not acquire or resell that work.

**[analysis] The only plausible retail argument is the “customer data” argument:** ADOR could contend that the provider pays $25, $75, or $199 and in return receives electronically delivered tangible personal property consisting of a homeowner's identity/contact information. Arizona's statutory definition of tangible personal property is unusually broad: A.R.S. §42-5001(21) includes property perceptible to the senses, and Arizona courts have applied that language to software and other nontraditional property. citeturn41view7turn34view2

**[analysis] I nevertheless think the retail argument is weak on the stated facts because the object of this particular transaction is the successful introduction, not acquisition of a data product.** There is no searchable lead database, bulk list, subscription, data export product, per-record catalog, or resale right. The name/address exists only because Ask Hava has already performed the matching process and the homeowner has independently selected that particular business.

A useful Arizona analogy is *Val-Pak East Valley, Inc. v. Arizona Department of Revenue*, discussed by the Court of Appeals in *ADP*. **[settled] In *Val-Pak*, the court dealt with the line between selling tangible property and providing an advertising service; *ADP* summarizes the principle that property can be merely incidental where the service is the primary object of the transaction.** citeturn35view0

**Retail conclusion — [analysis]: no, with roughly 85% confidence, provided Ask Hava does not sell or market the product as a “lead,” “lead record,” “customer data,” or other data product.**

### Personal-property rental and digital access

This is the classification deserving the most attention.

**[settled] A.R.S. §42-5071 imposes TPT on the business of leasing or renting tangible personal property for consideration.** Arizona Administrative Code R15-5-1503(B), quoted and applied by the Court of Appeals in *ADP*, likewise addresses leasing or renting tangible personal property in Arizona. citeturn34view2

**[settled] In *ADP, LLC v. ADOR/Phoenix*, 254 Ariz. 417 (App. 2023), ADP licensed its eTime web application to Maricopa County. County employees logged into and directly operated software configured for the County. The Court of Appeals held both that the software was tangible personal property under A.R.S. §42-5001(21) and that the transaction constituted a rental under §42-5071.** citeturn34view2

**[settled] *ADP* is particularly important because physical possession or downloading software was not required.** The court focused on possession and control at the customer-interface level. Individual credentials, customer-specific configuration, and the customer's ability to operate the application were sufficient even though ADP maintained the servers, source code, and software itself outside Arizona. citeturn35view0

**[settled] The court also rejected the argument that ADP was merely continuing to provide a nontaxable HR service through automation.** It reasoned that customers now directly used software that automated work ADP once performed manually. Separately stated software charges were important to the analysis. citeturn35view0

That is potentially dangerous precedent for any heavily automated Arizona platform, but it does **not** automatically mean every AI-powered service is a taxable software rental.

**[settled] The same *ADP* opinion cited cases going the other direction.** In *Jones Outdoor Advertising, Inc. v. ADOR*, customers received the right to have messages displayed but did not possess or control the billboard; in *Energy Squared, Inc. v. ADOR*, tanning-salon customers did not exercise sufficient exclusive control over the equipment, so the activity was treated as a service rather than a rental. citeturn35view0

**[analysis] That distinction strongly favors Ask Hava so long as the AI and platform remain tools that Ask Hava itself uses to perform the introduction service.** A contractor is not paying monthly to take possession or control of Ask Hava's matching software. It is not configuring an Ask Hava AI instance, renting compute capacity, buying seats, paying for dashboard access, or purchasing a period of platform usage. It pays only after a homeowner chooses it.

**[analysis] This distinction becomes materially weaker if Ask Hava later charges contractors subscriptions for dashboards, AI quoting tools, CRM functionality, searchable lead databases, or software seats.** Under *ADP*, those would need their own TPT analysis rather than automatically being treated as part of the nontaxable introduction service. citeturn34view2

### LR 21-003 and the special danger of digital data

**[settled] ADOR Private Taxpayer Ruling LR 21-003, issued in May 2021 and still listed in ADOR's legal-research materials, took an aggressive position on digital data.** The taxpayer compiled information from numerous sources into a continually updated repository; customers paid subscriptions for remote access to that data even though they did not receive access to the taxpayer's underlying software. ADOR concluded that temporary use of the digital information constituted taxable rental of tangible personal property. citeturn32search2turn32search18

**[settled] The published description of LR 21-003 reports that customers could access, filter, and extract information from the repository and that ADOR applied “dominant purpose” and “common understanding” concepts in deciding that access to the data—not the taxpayer's work collecting it—was what customers were really buying.** citeturn34view1turn32search7

### Prime contracting

### Online marketplace and remote-seller rules

**[settled] The current marketplace-facilitator definition in A.R.S. §42-5001 is much narrower than the everyday meaning of “online marketplace.”** A marketplace facilitator facilitates a **retail sale of tangible personal property** by listing or advertising the property and, directly or indirectly, **collecting the purchaser's payment and transmitting it to the marketplace seller**. citeturn41view7

**[analysis] Ask Hava fails both critical parts of that definition.** The transaction being facilitated is principally a service contract between homeowner and contractor, and Ask Hava never collects or transmits the homeowner's payment for the underlying job. Stripe's processing of the contractor's separate Ask Hava introduction fee does not turn Ask Hava into the processor of the homeowner-to-contractor transaction.

**[settled] A.R.S. §42-5001 also defines a “remote seller” as a person selling products for delivery into Arizona without the physical presence or other Arizona licensing connection described in the statute.** Ask Hava itself is an Arizona business operating from Lake Havasu City, so the remote-seller concept is not the appropriate framework for its own introduction-fee revenue. citeturn41view7

**[settled] ADOR's LR 17-011, dated July 26, 2017, addresses the “Taxability of the operator of an online marketplace.”** But that ruling predates Arizona's modern marketplace-facilitator regime; ADOR's current marketplace-facilitator requirements took effect in 2019. The current statutory definition should therefore drive a 2026 analysis rather than treating LR 17-011 as if it were the present marketplace statute. citeturn37search0turn41view7

**Marketplace-facilitator conclusion — [analysis]: no, approximately 95% confidence.**

## Lake Havasu City, advertising, licensing, and the data issue

Arizona cities use the Model City Tax Code framework, and that creates two local issues that deserve separate treatment from state TPT.

### Lake Havasu's §450 “licensing for use”

**[settled] Model City Tax Code §450 taxes gross income from the business of leasing, renting, or “licensing for use” tangible personal property.** That wording is broader on its face than merely asking whether there has been a traditional physical rental. citeturn40search3turn37search1

**[settled] *ADP* applied Phoenix's version of MCTC §450 and held eTime taxable.** Phoenix §14-450 covered leasing, licensing for use, or renting tangible personal property, and the court emphasized that ADP's agreement expressly granted Maricopa County a software license. citeturn35view0

**[settled] The Model City Tax Code also contains specific computer-hardware/software/data provisions, including treatment of charges for prewritten programs sold, rented, leased, or licensed.** citeturn40search22

**[analysis] This makes your contractual vocabulary materially important.** The contract should not say the contractor pays an introduction fee in return for a “license to use customer data,” “license to use the lead,” or “license to access Ask Hava software.” Those phrases unnecessarily move the transaction toward the exact statutory language in §450 and the reasoning used in *ADP*. citeturn35view0turn40search3

**[analysis] It is much better for Ask Hava's software to be the mechanism Ask Hava uses to perform the service rather than the thing the contractor is buying.** Contractor accounts and dashboards can exist, but access should remain incidental to participating in Ask Hava; the $25/$75/$199 amount should not be described or calculated as consideration for a software license, number of users, period of access, data volume, or platform features.

## Filing exposure, safest route, and current rate

### Do you need a TPT license or returns if the fee is nontaxable?

**[analysis] If Ask Hava engages only in the introduction activity described here and that activity is outside all state and city taxable classifications, then there is no TPT liability to report merely because Ask Hava is an Arizona LLC.** A TPT license is associated with engaging in activities on which transaction privilege tax is imposed; it is not a general substitute for forming an LLC. Arizona's licensing statute separately governs state and municipal TPT licenses. citeturn21search1turn22search9

**[analysis] I would therefore not obtain a TPT license solely on the theory that “maybe the fee is taxable” and then ignore filing obligations.** Once a taxpayer is registered and required to file, failing to file required TPT returns can itself create minimum penalties even when little tax is due. citeturn41view8

**[settled] For TPT returns, A.R.S. §42-1125(X) currently imposes a late-filing penalty of 4.5% of tax due or $25, whichever is greater, for each month or fraction of a month, subject to the statutory maximum of 25% of the tax or $100, whichever is greater.** citeturn41view8

### The statute-of-limitations problem you identified is real

**[settled] A.R.S. §42-1104 generally gives ADOR a four-year assessment period tied to filing of a return, but expressly permits assessment “at any time” for failure to file a return for Title 42 taxes other than the income/withholding exceptions identified in that statute.** Thus, if Ask Hava should have been filing TPT returns but never filed them, the ordinary four-year clock does not protect those unfiled periods. citeturn1view0

**[analysis] That does not mean a business must file a return for a tax it genuinely does not owe.** The danger is retrospective: if Ask Hava operates for six years without filing because it believes introduction fees are exempt, and ADOR later successfully determines the fees actually fell under a taxable classification, the absence of returns leaves much more historical exposure than a normal four-year audit period.

## Plain-English conclusion and what to do

**[analysis] In plain English:** Ask Hava looks much more like a **referral/introduction service** than a taxable retailer, software rental company, data seller, contractor, or marketplace facilitator.
